The Systems Engineering Process

Simple versionEvery engineering project follows the same 5 steps, in order: figure out the problem, come up with and design a solution, plan how to build it, actually build it, then check if it worked.

Every project in this study — no matter the system — moves through the same five activities. It isn't linear: you constantly loop back to re-test, re-plan and re-design as you learn more.

Control, Integration & Feedback

Simple versionOpen-loop = it just does its thing and doesn't check the result (a toaster running for a set time). Closed-loop = it checks the result and corrects itself (a thermostat that keeps checking the room temperature).

An open-loop system just acts on its input and hopes for the best — a toaster runs for a fixed time regardless of how the toast actually looks. A closed-loop system adds a sensor that measures the output and feeds it back to adjust the process — a thermostat checks the room temperature and keeps correcting. Toggle the loop below.

Control

Managing and directing a system's behaviour to achieve a desired outcome — e.g. a thermostat turning a heater on or off.

Integration

Combining different parts of a system so they work together seamlessly — e.g. lights, thermostat and security camera all responding to one "good night" voice command.

Feedback

Using information about a system's output to make adjustments — e.g. cruise control constantly checking speed and adjusting the throttle.

Circuits: Series, Parallel & Voltage Dividers

Simple versionIn a series circuit, everything is in one loop, so the same current flows everywhere. In a parallel circuit, there are multiple paths, so the current splits up.

The rule students misquote most: in series, current is constant and voltage splits; in parallel, voltage is constant and current splits. Watch the dots (current) and the bars (voltage) to see why.

Series — current constant, voltage splits

One path for current — every electron that flows through R1 must also flow through R2. The supply voltage is shared between the two resistors in proportion to their resistance.

Parallel — voltage constant, current splits

Two paths — each resistor sees the full supply voltage independently, but the total current splits between the branches according to each branch's resistance.

Diodes & Rectification

Simple versionA diode is a one-way valve for electricity — current can only flow through it in one direction, like a check valve in plumbing.

A diode only lets current through in one direction. Wire four of them into a diamond — a bridge rectifier — and an AC supply that swings positive and negative comes out the other side always pushing the same way. The waveform below is drawn in full so you can see the whole AC cycle and the resulting DC output at once, synced to the live current path.

Diode

Only allows current one way. Silicon diodes drop ~0.7 V, germanium ~1 V, across them while conducting.

LED / Photodiode

LED emits light when forward-biased current flows. A photodiode does the reverse — light striking it generates or enables current.

Zener diode

Designed to conduct in reverse once a specific breakdown voltage is reached — used deliberately for voltage regulation.

Transistors

Simple versionA transistor is an electronic switch. A tiny current at one pin (the base) controls whether a much bigger current can flow through the other two pins.

A transistor is a current-controlled switch (or amplifier): a small signal at the base terminal controls a much larger current flowing between collector and emitter. Slide the base voltage and watch which region the transistor is operating in.

0.00 V
CUT-OFF ACTIVE SATURATION

Reading the regions

Cut-off — base voltage below ~0.7 V threshold. No base current flows, so no collector current flows either. The switch is fully off.

Active — base current is flowing and collector current is proportional to it (Ic = β×Ib). This is where a transistor behaves as an amplifier.

Saturation — the base current is large enough that the transistor is fully on; collector current can't increase further no matter how much more base current is pushed in.

IE = IC + IB

Choosing NPN vs PNP: NPN turns on with a positive base voltage relative to the emitter and is the default choice for switching a load to ground. PNP turns on when the base is pulled low relative to the emitter — used when the load needs to switch to the positive supply rail (high-side switching).

Capacitors

Simple versionA capacitor is like a tiny, fast rechargeable battery — it stores a small amount of charge and can release it very quickly.

A capacitor stores charge temporarily (Q = CV). It blocks steady DC once fully charged but lets AC through — useful for smoothing, timing and filtering. Charging and discharging both follow the same exponential curve, just mirrored.

Time constant τ = — — the time to reach ~63% of full charge (or fall to ~37% on discharge).

After about 5τ, a capacitor is considered fully charged or discharged.

What's physically happening inside it

Two conductive plates face each other with an insulating gap between them. Charging pushes electrons onto one plate (making it negative) while pulling them off the other (leaving it positive) — no charge actually crosses the gap. The build-up of opposite charges creates an electric field between the plates, and that field is where the energy is stored.

Voltage regulator

Fixed regulators (e.g. 7805) output one set voltage regardless of input fluctuation. Variable regulators let you dial in the output via an external resistor/potentiometer.

Integrated circuit (IC)

A miniaturised circuit — including microcontrollers — packaged as a single component. Represented in schematics as a rectangle with numbered pins (a dual inline package, DIP), and can be modelled with digital simulation tools before being physically built.

Electrical safety limit: under the Electricity Safety Act 1998, students may only work on circuits at or below 50 V AC or 120 V ripple-free DC — anything above that requires a licensed electrician.

Motors, Solenoids, Generators & Switching

Simple versionMotors turn electricity into movement. Generators do the reverse — movement into electricity. Solenoids and relays are just switches/movers controlled by electromagnets.

Devices that convert electrical energy to motion (or the reverse), and the components used to switch and control them — all explicitly named in the study design's component list.

DC motor (brushed)

Current through a coil in a magnetic field produces a turning force (torque). A commutator/brushes reverse current direction every half-turn to keep it spinning. Simple, cheap, but brushes wear out.

Brushless DC motor

Electronic switching (not physical brushes) energises coils in sequence around a permanent-magnet rotor. Longer lifespan and more efficient, but needs a driver circuit/controller.

Stepper motor

Rotates in fixed, discrete steps as coils are energised in sequence — precise positioning without feedback. Uni-polar steppers use centre-tapped coils (current only ever flows one way per coil); bi-polar steppers reverse current direction through each coil for more torque, needing an H-bridge driver.

Servo motor

A DC motor plus a built-in feedback sensor (potentiometer/encoder) and control circuit, so it can be commanded to hold a precise angular position — a self-contained closed-loop system.

AC motor

Driven directly by an alternating current, using the changing field to induce rotation (e.g. induction motors) — common in mains-powered appliances and industrial equipment.

Solenoid

A coil that produces linear (not rotary) motion when energised, moving a metal plunger. Push types extend the plunger out; pull types retract it in; dual solenoids can do both depending on which winding is energised.

Generator / Dynamo

The reverse of a motor — mechanical rotation induces an EMF (electromagnetic induction). A dynamo produces DC output; an alternator produces AC output.

Relay

An electromagnet that pulls a mechanical switch contact — lets a small control current switch a much larger load current. Specified by normally open (NO) or normally closed (NC) contacts, plus a voltage and current rating for both the coil and the switched contacts.

Switches

Momentary switches only stay actuated while pressed (NO or NC); toggle switches stay in position until moved again. Poles/throws describe how many circuits are switched and how many positions each pole has (SPST, SPDT, DPST, DPDT). Switches can be single or ganged (multiple switches operated together), and include specialised types like micro, reed (magnetically actuated) and tilt ball switches.

Motion & Free Body Diagrams

Simple versionObjects move in 4 basic ways: straight-line (linear), spinning (rotary), back-and-forth in a line (reciprocating), or swinging (oscillating). A free body diagram is just a picture of every force pushing or pulling on an object.

Four categories of mechanical motion recur throughout the course, plus one tool that applies to nearly every mechanical question: the free body diagram.

Linear

Motion in a straight line.

Rotary

Motion around a fixed axis, continuously.

Reciprocating

Straight-line motion that repeats back and forth.

Oscillating

Back-and-forth motion around a pivot, like a pendulum.

Tension

A pulling/stretching force along a member, e.g. a rope or cable under load.

Compression

A pushing/squeezing force along a member, e.g. a column supporting a roof.

Torsion & shear

Torsion is a twisting force about an axis (e.g. a drive shaft). Shear is a force that causes layers of a material to slide past each other, like scissors cutting paper.

Hydraulics & Pneumatics

Simple versionBecause liquid can't be squeezed smaller, pushing on it in one spot pushes out everywhere else with equal pressure — that's how a small hydraulic piston can lift something huge on the other end.

Water (or oil) is incompressible; Pascal's Law says pressure applied anywhere in a sealed fluid is transmitted equally throughout it. Because pressure is the same everywhere, a small piston pushing into a larger one multiplies force. Pneumatics uses compressed air instead — faster and cleaner, but less precise and less powerful than hydraulics.

Ethical Design, Systems Production & Risk

Simple versionGood engineers don't just make something that works — they think about who it affects, keep people safe while building and using it, and keep proper records of what they did and why.

A system isn't finished when it works — it's finished when it works responsibly, and when the process used to build it is documented and repeatable. This section covers the "Ethical Systems Design" and "Systems Production" areas of study.

Environmental

Resource selection, energy use and end-of-life impact — designing so the system doesn't deplete resources or damage ecosystems across its lifecycle.

Economic

Efficient use of resources to sustain value over time — comparing material costs, and weighing present vs future costs across the system's lifecycle.

Social

Access to human rights, education and community connection — stewardship that supports the wellbeing of current and future generations.

Design thinking strategies

Critical — evaluating ideas rigorously against evidence, constraints and criteria; questioning assumptions.

Creative — generating a wide range of novel ideas and options before narrowing down.

Speculative — imagining future scenarios and "what if" possibilities to explore implications beyond the immediate brief.

Hierarchy of Controls

When managing a hazard, work from the top down — elimination is always preferred over relying on PPE.

Standards, documentation & open source

Australian and international standards, specifications, data sheets, safety data sheets (SDS) and technical manuals define expected performance, materials and safe practice — designers reference them to justify decisions and ensure compliance.

Open-source models (hardware and software) let designers build on others' work — this speeds development, but requires fair acknowledgment of the original creators/licence terms.

Risk assessment & management

Risk is managed at every stage of production and intended use — not just once at the start. A typical process: identify hazards → assess likelihood and consequence → apply hierarchy-of-controls treatments → record and review. This sits alongside OHS compliance when selecting and using components, materials and tools.

Production, diagnostics & evaluation

Production processes — simple operations (e.g. cutting, soldering a single joint) and complex operations (e.g. multi-stage PCB assembly and testing), carried out against a work plan.

Diagnostics & maintenance — using multimeters/oscilloscopes and inspection to measure, test and find faults; ongoing maintenance and repair procedures keep a system performing to spec.

Evaluation — interpreting measurements and criteria to judge whether the system met its brief, and to identify improvements for the next iteration.

Project management: agile production

Rather than a rigid, single-pass plan, production is managed with agile processes: iteration (repeating and refining), adaptability and flexibility (responding to problems as they arise) and continuous refinement — supported by work plans and ongoing records of evidence (logbooks, photos, test data) that justify decisions and modifications made along the way.

Clean Energy Technologies

Simple versionEnergy sources are either non-renewable (will run out — coal, oil, gas, nuclear) or renewable (won't run out — sun, wind, water, heat from the earth). Every source loses some energy at each step from source to your wall socket.

This area is separately assessed coursework. The core question throughout: where does the energy come from, how efficiently is it converted and delivered, and what does that cost environmentally, economically and socially across the whole life cycle?

Non-renewable sources

Fossil fuels — coal, oil and natural gas — remain the main source of global energy supply and electricity production, but release stored carbon when burned. Nuclear energy splits (fissions) heavy atoms to release heat, generating electricity without direct carbon emissions but producing radioactive waste.

Renewable sources

Solar (photovoltaic or thermal), wind (turbines), hydro (moving/falling water), tidal and wave (ocean movement), geothermal (heat from the Earth) and biomass (organic matter burned or converted to fuel).

Energy life cycle

Every energy source moves through the same stages, and losses/impacts occur at each one: harnessing or extraction → generation → conversion → transportation → storage → use.

Cradle-to-cradle (C2C) analysis

A sustainability framework that looks beyond "cradle to grave" disposal, aiming for materials to be fully reused, recycled or safely returned to the environment at end-of-life rather than becoming waste — considered alongside the environmental, economic and social pillars above when judging a technology's impact across its whole life cycle.

Wind turbine systems

Blades capture kinetic energy from moving air and turn a generator (often via a gearbox). Efficiency depends on blade design, wind speed and siting; output is intermittent (depends on wind).

Solar systems

PV cells convert light directly to DC electricity via the photovoltaic effect; solar thermal systems instead use sunlight to heat a fluid. Efficiency depends on cell material, angle/orientation and shading.

Hybrid fuel cells & combinations

Fuel cells convert a fuel's chemical energy directly to electricity (often via hydrogen) with water as the main by-product. Hybrid systems combine multiple technologies (e.g. solar + battery storage) to smooth out intermittency.

Batteries: mechanical vs chemical

Chemical batteries (e.g. lithium-ion) store energy in reversible chemical reactions — high energy density, but resource-intensive to manufacture and with end-of-life recycling challenges. Mechanical storage (e.g. pumped hydro, flywheels, compressed air) stores energy as gravitational, kinetic or pressure potential — generally lower energy density but often more durable and easier to recycle.

Efficiency of conversion

Every conversion step loses some energy (usually as waste heat) — factors affecting overall efficiency include the technology itself, transmission distance/losses, storage losses, and how well supply is matched to demand. This is the same efficiency formula used throughout the course: efficiency (%) = useful energy output ÷ total energy input × 100%.

New & Emerging Technologies

Simple versionFor any new piece of technology, ask the same 5 questions: what is it, how does it work, why did it appear now, what's good about it, and what's bad about it.

This area is also separately assessed coursework — you'll typically research a specific real technology, but the study design's framing questions are consistent across any example you pick.

What to know about any new/emerging technology

How the product, component or process actually works and what it's used for; the drivers behind its development — new discoveries, new materials, the convergence of previously separate technologies, and new manufacturing methods/processes.

Judging impact

Weigh up positive and negative impacts (economic, environmental, social) and the technology's future potential — including how it might improve efficiency, sustainability or risk management in systems engineering processes more broadly.

Exam-ready structure for a written answer: (1) what it is / how it works, (2) why it emerged now (drivers), (3) positive impacts, (4) negative impacts / risks, (5) future potential.

The centripetal force always points toward the centre of the circle — it's what keeps the object turning instead of flying off in a straight line (Newton's 1st Law).

Banked track

On a banked (tilted) curve, part of the normal force itself points toward the centre of the circle, so friction doesn't have to supply all the centripetal force — that's why banking lets vehicles corner faster safely. At the "design speed" for a given bank angle θ, friction isn't needed at all: tanθ = v²/(rg).

Vertical circular motion

Going around a vertical loop (e.g. a roller coaster or a ball on a string), gravity contributes to the centripetal force at the top and opposes it at the bottom:

Top: Fnet = FN + mg = mv²/r  |  Bottom: Fnet = FN − mg = mv²/r

At the top, normal force can drop to zero at minimum speed (vmin = √(gr)) — any slower and the object can't maintain the circular path and falls away from the track.

Satellite orbits

A satellite's weight is its centripetal force — gravity supplies exactly what's needed to keep it turning: GMm/r² = mv²/r = m(4π²r)/T². This is why orbit height and orbital period are linked: a higher orbit means a slower, longer-period orbit.

F = k q₁q₂ / r²   |   E = F/q

Like charges repel, opposite charges attract — same inverse-square shape as gravity, but gravity is always attractive while electric force can push or pull.

F = qvB (force on a moving charge)   |   F = BIL (force on a current-carrying wire)

Right-hand slap rule (for positive charges): fingers point along v, curl toward B, thumb gives F. Flip for negative charges.

Simple DC motor

A single coil (several loops of wire) sits free to rotate inside a uniform magnetic field. Current flowing through the coil experiences a force (F = nIlB) on each side, and because the two sides carry current in opposite directions, the forces form a couple that turns the coil.

A split-ring commutator reverses the current in the coil every half-turn, so the torque keeps acting in the same rotational sense instead of the coil oscillating back and forth and stalling.

F = nIlB  (n = number of loops, I = current, l = conductor length, B = field strength)

Torque increases with more current, a stronger external field, or more loops of wire — but the torque is not constant through a rotation: it's greatest when the coil plane is parallel to the field, and zero when perpendicular to it.

Particle accelerators (incl. synchrotrons)

Charged particles are accelerated using the two field effects covered in this section, combined:

  • Linear acceleration — a uniform electric field does work on the charge, speeding it up (same F = qE physics as above).
  • Direction change — a uniform magnetic field bends the particle's path without changing its speed (F = qvB, always perpendicular to v), which is what keeps particles moving in the circular/near-circular path of a synchrotron while separate sections re-accelerate them.

Modelled as uniform circular motion: qvB = mv²/r, so a stronger field or faster particle needs a larger radius to match.

Electromagnetic Induction

Simple versionIf you move a magnet near a coil of wire (or spin a coil inside a magnet), you generate electricity, with no battery involved. This is how basically all the world's electricity is made.

Move a magnet near a coil and you induce an EMF — this is how every generator on the planet makes electricity. Faraday's Law says the size of the EMF depends on how fast the magnetic flux through the coil is changing; Lenz's Law says the induced current always flows in the direction that opposes that change.

EMF = -N × (ΔΦ / Δt)
5

Faster motion → faster-changing flux → bigger EMF (bigger galvanometer deflection). Reversing direction reverses the induced current (Lenz's Law).

Click a button to move the magnet.

AC generator — EMF over one rotation

Spin a coil inside a magnetic field at constant speed and the EMF it produces traces a sine wave — maximum when the coil cuts through field lines fastest (coil plane parallel to the field), zero when it's momentarily not cutting any lines (coil plane perpendicular to the field).

Described with reference to frequency, period, amplitude, peak-to-peak voltage (Vp–p) and peak-to-peak current (Ip–p).

RMS voltage & current

Vrms = Vpeak / √2   |   Irms = Ipeak / √2

An alternating voltage's RMS value is the equivalent constant DC voltage that would deliver the same power to a resistive component. Standard "230 V mains" is already an RMS figure — the actual peak voltage swings considerably higher (about 325 V).

DC generator vs alternator

Both convert rotational mechanical energy to electrical energy via induction, but differ in how the rotating coil connects to the external circuit: a DC generator uses a split-ring commutator (like a DC motor in reverse) to keep the output current flowing one direction; an alternator uses continuous slip rings, so the output faithfully follows the coil's sinusoidal EMF as true AC.

Photovoltaic cells & inverters

A PV cell generates DC directly from light. Since household appliances and the grid run on AC, an inverter converts the cell's DC output to AC before it can be used in the home or exported to the grid (the internal semiconductor/circuit detail isn't required — just that the conversion is necessary).

Transmission losses

Power lines have resistance, so some transmitted power is lost as heat: Ploss = I²R. Because loss scales with the square of current, power companies step voltage up (and current down) for long-distance transmission using transformers, then step it back down near homes — the same power delivered at a much lower current means far less lost to line resistance.

Waves, Light & Quanta

Simple versionLight sometimes behaves like a wave (spreading out, overlapping, cancelling out) and sometimes behaves like a stream of tiny particles (photons). Both descriptions are needed, and neither one alone is the full story.

Unit 4 asks how two seemingly contradictory models — waves and particles — can both be needed to explain light and matter. It starts with ordinary wave behaviour, then shows where that model breaks down.

v = f × λ
4 Hz

Wave speed is set by the medium, not the source — increase the frequency and the wavelength must shrink to compensate, keeping v constant.

Standing waves

When a travelling wave reflects back on itself (e.g. off a fixed end), the original and reflected waves superpose. At certain frequencies this produces a standing wave — a pattern that appears to stay still, with fixed nodes (no displacement) and antinodes (maximum displacement) rather than a travelling crest.

This course only requires standing waves with nodes at both ends (like a guitar string fixed at both ends).

Diffraction

Diffraction is the directional spread of a wave after passing a gap or obstacle. How much it spreads depends on the ratio of wavelength to gap/obstacle width, λ/w:

  • λ/w small (gap much wider than wavelength) → little spreading, the wave mostly travels straight through
  • λ/w large (gap comparable to or smaller than wavelength) → strong spreading in all directions

This sets a practical limit on imaging: you can't resolve detail smaller than roughly the wavelength of the wave you're imaging with — one reason electron microscopes (tiny de Broglie wavelength) resolve far finer detail than light microscopes.

Light passing through two narrow slits spreads out (diffracts) and overlaps. Where a crest meets a crest, the waves add up — constructive interference, a bright fringe. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel — destructive interference, a dark fringe. This pattern only makes sense if light behaves as a wave.

Below the threshold frequency, no electrons are ejected no matter how bright the light is — a result the wave model can't explain, but the particle (photon) model can: each photon carries a fixed energy hf, and a photon below the threshold energy just can't knock an electron out, regardless of how many photons arrive. Increasing intensity at a fixed frequency above threshold increases the number of photoelectrons ejected, not their maximum kinetic energy.

λ = h / p = h / (mv)   |   pphoton = h / λ

De Broglie proposed that if light (a wave) can behave like particles, matter (particles) should also have a wave nature. Every moving object has a wavelength — it's just unimaginably small for anything bigger than an electron.

electron (9.11×10⁻³¹ kg)
2 ×10⁶ m/s

A photon of the same wavelength as a moving particle carries momentum p = h/λ — comparing photon and matter momentum at equal wavelengths is a standard exam-style calculation.

Evidence for duality: electron diffraction patterns (electrons fired at a crystal produce ring patterns just like X-ray diffraction) show matter's wave-like nature. The single-photon / single-electron double-slit experiment — sending particles through one at a time — still builds up an interference pattern over many trials, showing each individual particle behaves as though it interferes with itself as a wave, yet arrives at the detector as a single localised particle. This dual behaviour is the strongest evidence that light and matter are neither purely wave nor purely particle.

Atomic absorption & emission line spectra

Electrons in an atom occupy discrete (quantised) energy states. An electron can jump to a higher state by absorbing a photon, or drop to a lower state by emitting one — in both cases the photon's energy exactly matches the difference between the two states:

E = hf = hc/λ = Ehigh − Elow

An emission spectrum (e.g. from a metal vapour lamp) shows bright lines at exactly the wavelengths the atom can emit. An absorption spectrum shows dark lines at the same wavelengths, where atoms in a cooler gas have absorbed light of just those energies from a continuous source passing through. Because each element's energy states are unique, its line spectrum acts as a fingerprint — which is how astronomers identify the composition of distant stars.

This quantisation is a unifying idea across the topic: it's the same reasoning that explains why the photoelectric effect has a threshold frequency, and it applies equally to explaining the structure of atoms.

Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity

Simple versionAt everyday speeds, time and distance feel fixed and the same for everyone. Einstein showed that once you're moving close to the speed of light, time actually runs slower for you and distances actually shrink, compared to someone standing still.

Classical (Newtonian) mechanics quietly assumes that time and length are absolute — the same for every observer. Einstein showed that breaks down at speeds approaching the speed of light, and rebuilt mechanics from two postulates instead.

The two postulates

1. The laws of physics are the same in all inertial (non-accelerated) frames of reference — there's no experiment you can run inside a closed, smoothly-moving lab that tells you your own speed.

2. The speed of light in a vacuum, c, has the same value for every observer, regardless of their own motion or the motion of the light source.

Postulate 2 is the strange one: unlike a ball thrown from a moving car, light doesn't go any faster if its source is moving toward you. Something else has to give — and what gives is the assumption that time and length are the same for everyone.

The Michelson-Morley experiment

Before Einstein, physicists expected light's speed to vary depending on Earth's motion through a hypothesised medium (the "aether"), the way sound's measured speed depends on the observer's motion through air. Michelson and Morley very precisely compared light's speed in perpendicular directions and found no difference at all — a null result. This is interpreted as strong evidence for Einstein's postulates: light's speed simply doesn't depend on the observer's motion, so there's no aether to detect.

Proper time & proper length

Proper time, t₀ — the time interval between two events as measured in the one reference frame where both events happen at the same point in space (e.g. a clock you're carrying with you).

Proper length, L₀ — the length of an object as measured in the frame of reference in which the object is at rest.

Any other observer, moving relative to these frames, measures a longer time interval (time dilation) and a shorter length (length contraction) for the same events/object.

Real-world examples

Muons created in the upper atmosphere have such short half-lives that, classically, almost none should survive the trip to Earth's surface. Time dilation (from our frame, the fast-moving muon's internal "clock" runs slow) lets far more of them reach the ground than non-relativistic physics predicts.

Particle accelerators must be designed accounting for length contraction and relativistic mass-energy effects at the very high speeds particles reach — classical mechanics alone gives the wrong answers for accelerator geometry and energy requirements.

GPS satellites move fast enough, and sit in a different gravitational potential, that their onboard clocks drift out of sync with ground clocks. Time signals must be corrected for these relativistic effects or GPS positioning would accumulate significant error within minutes.

Mass-energy equivalence

E0 = mc²   |   Etot = Ek + E0 = γmc²   |   Ek = (γ − 1)mc²

Mass and energy are, in a sense, the same thing viewed two ways. E₀ = mc² is an object's "rest energy" — energy it has purely by virtue of having mass, even standing still. Because c² is enormous, converting even a tiny amount of mass releases a huge amount of energy.

Applications: mass conversion in the Sun (nuclear fusion converts a small fraction of mass to energy, powering the Sun's output), positron-electron annihilation (matter and antimatter fully converting to energy/photons), and nuclear transformations in particle accelerators (the specific nuclear processes aren't required in detail — just that mass and energy interconvert according to E = mc²).
